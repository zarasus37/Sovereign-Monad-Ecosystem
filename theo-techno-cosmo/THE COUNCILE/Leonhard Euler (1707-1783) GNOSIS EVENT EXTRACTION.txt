## Contribution

I referred the Königsberg bridge problem to **geometry of position** and gave a general rule when a walk can cross each bridge once; I made the **function** the central object of analysis in the *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* (1748); I fixed the base **e** of natural logarithms as an infinite series and the exponential *e*^z as series; and I showed how **imaginary exponential quantities** reduce to the sine and cosine of real arcs — *e*^(+*v*√−1) = cos *v* + √−1 · sin *v*.

## Key Insight

Position without quantity yields a rule of parity for bridges; a function is an analytic expression of a variable; natural growth’s base *e* is a series; and the circle and the exponential meet when the exponent is imaginary.

## Event 01 — *Geometry of position*

**Title:** Another part of geometry, still quite unknown

**What I did / What was made:**
I opened the Königsberg paper by placing the problem under Leibniz’s *geometria situs* — determination of position without calculation of quantities.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> Besides that part of geometry which is concerned with quantities, and has at all times been studied with the greatest zeal, there is another part, still quite unknown, of which Leibnitz was the first to make mention and which he called *geometria situs*, the geometry of position. This part is defined by him as being concerned only with the determination of position, and with drawing out the properties of position; in which business quantities are not considered, nor is calculation with quantites employed. But what kinds of problem belong to this geometry of position, and what kind of method is suitable for solving them, is not adequately defined. So, since mention was recently made of a certain problem, which clearly had something to do with geometry, but was presented in such a way that it neither required the determination of quantities, nor did calculation with quantities help towards its solution, I did not hesitate to refer it to the geometry of position: especially because in its solution only position is considered; there is no need at all for [geometrical] calculation. Therefore I have decided to publish here my method that I have found for solving problems of this kind, as a typical example of the geometry of position.

— Leonhard Euler, “Solution of a problem in the geometry of position,” continuous opening. Latin *Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis*, *Commentarii academiae scientiarum Petropolitanae* **8** (1736/1741). English continuous republication of the paper.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Not magnitudes — position. Leibniz named the part; the problem fits it; I publish a method as example of that geometry.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Topology/graph theory seed stated as geometry without quantity.

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** Form of connection as object of knowledge, not only measure.
- **Technology:** Method for a class of position problems, not one civic puzzle alone.
- **Cosmology:** Space of relations among regions independent of metric size.

**Compressed Insight:**
*Geometria situs* concerns position alone — quantities not considered; the bridge problem belongs there.

**Confidence:** 0.99  
**Sources:** E53 English continuous §1.

## Event 02 — *Seven bridges of Königsberg*

**Title:** Whether each bridge once only

**What I did / What was made:**
I stated the problem of the seven bridges and the general question for any river and any number of bridges.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> This problem, then, which was described to me as quite well known, was as follows: At Königsberg in Prussia there is an island A called *der Kneiphof*, and the river around it is divided into two branches, as shown in the figure; and the branches of this river are crossed by seven bridges *a*, *b*, *c*, *d*, *e*, *f*, and *g*. The following question was now raised concerning these bridges: whether someone could arrange a walk in such a way as to travel over every bridge once and not more than once. Some people (I was told) deny that this is possible, and others doubt it; but nobody asserts it. From this I set myself the following quite general problem: whatever the form of the river and its distribution into branches, and whatever the number of bridges, to find whether it is possible for each bridge to be crossed only once, or not.

— Euler, geometry of position paper, §2 continuous.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Not only Königsberg — any form of waters and bridges. Possible or not, decided by method.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Local riddle raised to general positional problem.

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** Civic order of paths as lawful question.
- **Technology:** General problem statement before enumeration.
- **Cosmology:** Network of regions and crossings as pure form.

**Compressed Insight:**
Seven bridges — can each be crossed once only? And the same for any form of river and any number of bridges.

**Confidence:** 0.99  
**Sources:** E53 §2 continuous.

## Event 03 — *Letters for crossings*

**Title:** Notation AB, ABD, eight letters for seven bridges

**What I did / What was made:**
I based the method on denoting regions by capital letters and successive crossings by strings of those letters.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> Now my whole method is based on a suitable way of denoting individual crossings of bridges, in which I use capital letters A, B, C, D applied to the individual regions that are separated by the river. Thus if someone crosses from region A into region B by bridge *a* or *b*, I denote this crossing by the letters AB, of which the first shows the region from which the traveller left, and the second gives the region in which he arrives after crossing the bridge. If the traveller next goes out of region B into region D by bridge *f*, this crossing is represented by the letters BD; but these two crossings AB and BD, if made in succession, I denote by only three letters ABD, for the middle one B denotes both the region in which he arrived on the first crossing, and the region from which he left on the second crossing.
>
> Similarly, if the traveller continues from region D into region C by bridge *g*, these three crossings, made in succession, I shall denote by the four letters ABDC. … So then a crossing over seven bridges requires eight letters to represent it.
>
> In this notation I do not take account of the bridges by which the crossing is made, but if the same crossing from one region to another can be made by several bridges, it is just the same whichever one he crosses by, as long as he arrives in the designated region. From which it is understood that, if a walk across the seven bridges of the figure can be so arranged that each is crossed once and none twice, this walk can be represented by eight letters, and that those letters must be so arranged that the letters A and B occur together twice… Similarly the letters A and C must also occur together twice… next the letters A and D will occur together once; and similarly it is necessary that the letters B and D, as also C and D, shall occur together once.

— Euler, geometry of position paper, §§4–6 continuous.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Drop which bridge — keep which regions join. Seven bridges → eight letters with prescribed pair-counts. That is the whole walk.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Abstraction to combinatorial string — birth move of network mathematics.

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** Identity of path as succession of places.
- **Technology:** Letter calculus as instrument replacing exhaustive enumeration.
- **Cosmology:** Structure of connection independent of river scenery.

**Compressed Insight:**
Crossings denoted by letters of regions — seven bridges require eight letters with prescribed pairings.

**Confidence:** 0.99  
**Sources:** E53 §§4–6 continuous.

## Event 04 — *Cannot be achieved at all*

**Title:** Odd bridges and the letter count

**What I did / What was made:**
I proved Königsberg impossible: five bridges into A force letter A three times; three into each of B, C, D force each twice — impossible in eight letters — and gave the general rule by odd/even bridge counts.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> In order to find such a rule I consider a single region A, into which lead any number of bridges *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* etc. … If, say, three bridges *a*, *b*, *c* lead into region A, and the traveller crosses by all three, then in the record of his travels the letter A will occur twice, whether he starts his walk at A or not. In the same way if five bridges lead into A, in the record of crossing them all the letter A must occur three times. And if the number of bridges is any odd number, then if it is increased by one, its half will give the number of places in which the letter A must occur.
>
> In the case therefore of the bridges that have to be crossed at Königsberg, since five bridges *a*, *b*, *c*, *d*, *e* lead into the island A it is necessary that in the record of crossing by these bridges the letter A shall occur three times. Next the letter B, since three bridges lead into region B, must occur twice, similarly the letter D must occur twice, and again the letter C twice. Therefore in the series of eight letters by which the crossing of the seven bridges would have to be recorded, the letter A would have to be present three times, and each of the letters B, C, D twice; which in a series of eight letters cannot be done at all. From which it is clear that such a crossing of the seven Königsberg bridges cannot be achieved.
>
> …
>
> Therefore in any given case it will be very easy to decide straightaway whether a crossing by each bridge once only can be planned or not, with the help of the following rule. If there are more than two regions with an odd number of bridges leading into them, then it can safely be stated that there is no such crossing. And if there are exactly two regions with an odd number of bridges leading into them, then the crossing can be done, provided the walk is started in one of these two regions. Finally, if there is no region at all with an odd number of bridges leading into it, then the crossing can be planned in the desired way, and the start of the walking can be placed in any region. Therefore the rule just given fully satisfies the statement of the problem.

— Euler, geometry of position paper, §§8–9 and §20 continuous.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Odd degree forces letter frequency. Königsberg has four odd regions — more than two — so no tour. Zero or two odds: possible under start rules.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Euler’s theorem on paths in continuous early form (parity of incidences).

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** Impossibility as rigorous knowledge, not opinion.
- **Technology:** Decision rule before constructing the walk.
- **Cosmology:** Network law independent of particular map.

**Compressed Insight:**
More than two regions with an odd number of bridges — no such crossing; Königsberg has four odds — cannot be achieved at all.

**Confidence:** 0.99  
**Sources:** E53 §§8–9, §20 continuous.

## Event 05 — *A function of a variable quantity*

**Title:** Analytic expression as object of analysis

**What I did / What was made:**
In the *Introductio* I defined a function as an analytic expression composed of the variable and constants, and allowed the variable to include zero and complex numbers.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> A function of a variable quantity is an analytic expression composed in any way whatsoever of the variable quantity and numbers or constant quantities.
>
> […] Even zero and complex numbers are not excluded from the signification of a variable quantity.

— Leonhard Euler, *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* Book I (1748), Ch. 1 continuous. English John D. Blanton, *Introduction to Analysis of the Infinite* (Springer 1988); continuous scholarly carrier of Blanton’s translation of the Latin.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Analysis studies functions, not only curves of geometry. Variable ranges widely — even complex. Expression is the object.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Modern analysis’s central object fixed in continuous definition.

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** Variable magnitude under law of expression.
- **Technology:** Formula as portable object across problems.
- **Cosmology:** Quantity including complex in the domain of analysis.

**Compressed Insight:**
A function is an analytic expression of a variable quantity — and zero and complex are not excluded from the variable.

**Confidence:** 0.98  
**Sources:** *Introductio* I.1 Blanton continuous.

## Event 06 — *The symbol e*

**Title:** Base of natural or hyperbolic logarithms

**What I did / What was made:**
I chose the base so that *k* = 1, obtained the series for that base, named it *e*, and wrote *e*^z as infinite series.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> Since we are free to choose the base *a* for the system of logarithms, we now choose *a* in such a way that *k* = 1. Suppose now that *k* = 1; then the series found above in section 116, 1 + 1/1 + 1/(1·2) + 1/(1·2·3) + 1/(1·2·3·4) + ··· , is equal to *a*. If the terms are represented as decimal fractions and summed, we obtain the value for
>
> *a* = 2.71828182845904523536028··· .
>
> When this base is chosen, the logarithms are called natural or hyperbolic. The latter name is used since the quadrature of a hyperbola can be expressed through these logarithms. For the sake of brevity for this number 2.718281828459··· we will use the symbol *e*, which will denote the base of the natural or hyperbolic logarithms, which corresponds to the value *k* = 1, and *e* represents the sum of the infinite series 1 + 1/1 + 1/(1·2) + 1/(1·2·3) + 1/(1·2·3·4) + ··· .
>
> Natural logarithms have the property that the logarithm of 1 + *ω* is equal to *ω*, where *ω* is an infinitely small quantity. From this it follows that *k* = 1, and the natural logarithms of all numbers can be found. Let *e* stand for the number found above, then
>
> *e*^z = 1 + *z*/1 + *z*²/(1·2) + *z*³/(1·2·3) + *z*⁴/(1·2·3·4) + ··· ,
>
> and the natural logarithms themselves can be found from these series…

— Euler, *Introductio* Book I, Ch. 7 continuous (Blanton English; MathFest continuous carrier of §§122–123). Note: section numbers follow Blanton’s chapter structure.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Choose *k* = 1; the series *is* the base; call it *e*; then *e*^z is the matching series. Natural log of 1+*ω* is *ω* when *ω* is infinitely small.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Base of continuous growth fixed as series and symbol.

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** Natural measure of growth and hyperbole’s quadrature.
- **Technology:** Symbol *e* and series as calculational instruments.
- **Cosmology:** Exponential law as infinite sum of factorial denominators.

**Compressed Insight:**
*e* is the base of natural logarithms — sum of 1 + 1/1 + 1/(1·2) + 1/(1·2·3) + ··· — and *e*^z is the same series in powers of *z*.

**Confidence:** 0.99  
**Sources:** *Introductio* I Blanton Ch. 7 continuous.

## Event 07 — *Imaginary exponential quantities*

**Title:** *e*^(+*v*√−1) = cos *v* + √−1 · sin *v*

**What I did / What was made:**
From infinite forms of sine and cosine I reduced imaginary exponentials to real circular functions — and conversely.

**What I wrote / said (direct — full passage):**

> After logarithms and exponential quantities have been considered, circular arcs and the sines and cosines of these must be considered; not only because they constitute another kind of transcending quantity, but also because of the logarithms and exponentials of these that arise when they are involved with imaginary quantities, which will become clearer below.
>
> Therefore we may put the radius of the circle or the whole sine to be 1 and it is clear enough that the periphery of this circle cannot be expressed exactly in rational numbers; but by approximations the semi-circumference of this circle has been found… for which number for the sake of brevity I may write as *π*, thus so that there shall be *π* = semi-circumference of the circle, whose radius = 1…
>
> …
>
> But in the preceding chapter we have seen that
>
> (1 + *z*/*i*)^i = *e*^z
>
> with *e* denoting the base of hyperbolic logarithms; therefore for *z* in one part I write +*v*√−1 and for the other part −*v*√−1, and there becomes…
>
> From which it is understood, how imaginary exponential quantities may be reduced to the sine and cosine of real arcs. Truly there will be
>
> *e*^(+*v*√−1) = cos *v* + √−1 · sin *v*
>
> and
>
> *e*^(−*v*√−1) = cos *v* − √−1 · sin *v*.

— Euler, *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* Book I, Ch. 8 continuous (Ian Bruce English of the Latin §§126–138 sense-unit; formula as in Bruce §138). Modern *e*^{*iv*} = cos *v* + *i* sin *v* is the same with *i* = √−1.

**How I see it (from inside, in action):**
Circle and exponential meet through imaginary. *π* names the semi-circumference for radius 1. The exponential of ±*v*√−1 *is* cos ± √−1 sin.

**Why it qualifies as gnosis:**
Unification of circular and exponential transcendents under one analytic expression.

**Three-Domain Reading:**

- **Theology:** One law spanning growth and rotation.
- **Technology:** Imaginary unit as bridge between series and arc measures.
- **Cosmology:** Circle’s *π* and base *e* in one equation-family.

**Compressed Insight:**
Imaginary exponential quantities reduce to sine and cosine: *e*^(+*v*√−1) = cos *v* + √−1 · sin *v*.

**Confidence:** 0.99  
**Sources:** *Introductio* I.8 Bruce continuous §138 (with §126 *π* setup).
